Below is a deeper, problem-only articulation of what EKYAN is facing. I am deliberately not introducing solutions yet. The aim is to define the problem space precisely before deciding what should be built.
EKYAN: Detailed Problem Analysis
1. The central programme problem
EKYAN has shown that training, mentoring and local agripreneur support can create interest in agriculture and improve participant capability. The deeper problem is that knowledge and intention do not consistently translate into implementation, income and sustainable livelihoods.
A farmer can understand a recommended practice and genuinely intend to apply it, but still be prevented from doing so by:
unavailable inputs;
unaffordable inputs;
lack of working capital;
limited water or labour;
disease and production risks;
no confirmed buyer;
weak transport or distribution;
lack of timely technical support.
The programme therefore faces a knowledge-to-action gap.
The issue is not simply:
“Farmers need more training.”
It is:
“Farmers may have received the training, but the surrounding conditions required to implement it are not reliably available.”
The October programme material reportedly reflected more than 220,000 adoption intentions, while verification found that approximately 66% had fully adopted the practice. The difference suggests that intention alone is not a sufficient measure of programme success. It does not show whether the participant obtained the resources, support and market conditions needed to follow through.
2. Blockers are identified too late
The problem
Farmers and agripreneurs do not appear to have one consistent, real-time mechanism through which they can report:
what they are trying to implement;
what is preventing them from doing it;
how urgent the issue is;
what support they require;
whether previous support resolved the problem.
The programme has identified barriers through surveys, field visits and verification exercises. These methods are useful for evaluation, but they often reveal problems after the farmer has already delayed, abandoned or incorrectly implemented the practice.
Why this matters
Consider a farmer who learns that poultry must be vaccinated at a particular stage.
The farmer may later discover that:
the vaccine is unavailable locally;
the nearest supplier sells a quantity that is too large;
the farmer cannot afford the minimum pack;
the product requires refrigeration;
transport costs exceed the value of the order;
the farmer does not know which product is genuine;
the farmer cannot reach the agripreneur in time.
By the time a periodic survey records “lack of inputs” as the barrier, the vaccination window may have passed.
Deeper root cause
The missing element is not simply a communication channel. It is an accountable resolution structure.
Even if farmers can send a message, the system remains ineffective unless every reported issue has:
a responsible person or organisation;
a response deadline;
an escalation path;
a clear status;
proof that the issue was resolved;
confirmation that the farmer could proceed.
Without this structure, a reporting mechanism risks becoming another place where farmers describe problems without receiving help.
Programme consequence
Late blocker visibility causes:
lower adoption;
repeated problems across communities;
preventable production losses;
frustration and declining trust;
weak evidence about why interventions succeed or fail;
reactive rather than preventive programme management.
3. Input access is a coordination failure
The problem
Farmers may know which seeds, fertilisers, feed, vaccines, equipment or other inputs they need, but cannot necessarily obtain them:
at the correct time;
in sufficient quantity;
in an affordable pack size;
at an acceptable price;
from a trustworthy supplier;
within practical travelling distance;
with suitable payment terms.
The programme material describes last-mile input access as an important growth constraint and identifies reliable access to quality inputs at the correct time, quantity and price as an unresolved challenge.
Why common supplier models fail
From the farmer's perspective, the need may be small and urgent.
For example:
one farmer needs 50 vaccine doses;
another needs two bags of feed;
another needs 5 kg of certified seed.
From the supplier's perspective, rural distribution can be unattractive because:
individual orders are too small;
farmers are geographically dispersed;
last-mile transport is expensive;
demand is difficult to forecast;
customers may not be ready to pay simultaneously;
product expiry and storage risks are high;
there is no reliable local distribution point.
This means that demand exists, but the supplier cannot see it as a commercially viable opportunity.
Deeper root cause
The problem is fragmented and invisible demand.
Fifty farmers may collectively represent a strong order, but if each farmer approaches the market individually, the supplier sees fifty small, uncertain transactions rather than one organised demand cluster.
Additional quality problem
Input access is not only about availability and price. Farmers must also be protected from:
counterfeit seed;
expired vaccines;
inappropriate pesticides;
poor-quality feed;
unregistered products;
suppliers who fail to deliver;
products unsuitable for the local production context.
Therefore, “the lowest-priced supplier” cannot automatically be treated as the best supplier.
Programme consequence
Input failures create a broken chain:
Training completed → intention recorded → input unavailable → practice not implemented → expected outcome not achieved.
The programme may successfully deliver knowledge but still fail to achieve production, income or climate outcomes because the material requirement for implementation was unavailable.
4. Agripreneurs and farmers are financially invisible
The problem
Many agripreneurs and farmers operate genuine businesses but cannot present themselves to banks or formal finance providers in a format that supports conventional credit assessment.
They may lack:
formal financial statements;
consistent bookkeeping;
collateral;
payslips;
long credit histories;
registered contracts;
clearly separated personal and business accounts;
independently verifiable revenue records;
evidence of their customers and completed orders.
A bank may therefore classify them as high-risk, not necessarily because their businesses are unviable, but because the evidence needed to assess the businesses is missing or fragmented.
The underlying information asymmetry
The agripreneur may know:
how many farmers they serve;
how much feed or seed they sell;
how often customers return;
what they earn in a typical month;
which buyers normally purchase their produce;
how quickly they can repay after harvest.
But the lender cannot confidently verify this information.
This creates an information gap:
The business exists, but it is not sufficiently visible to the financial system.
Why ordinary credit assessment does not fit agriculture well
Agricultural businesses often have:
seasonal cash flow;
irregular income;
production cycles longer than one month;
weather-related volatility;
delayed buyer payments;
significant upfront input costs;
income concentrated around harvest or livestock-sale dates.
A conventional monthly repayment structure may therefore show a business as unaffordable even when it could repay successfully according to its production cycle.
The bookkeeping problem
Weak bookkeeping has two consequences.
For the agripreneur
The owner may not clearly know:
actual profit;
highest operating cost;
cash needed for the next cycle;
whether revenue is growing;
which customers or products are most profitable;
whether the business can safely take on debt.
For the lender
The bank cannot adequately assess:
repayment capacity;
existing obligations;
revenue stability;
customer concentration;
fulfilment reliability;
business growth;
the relationship between the requested loan and expected income.
Programme consequence
Promising agripreneurs may remain trapped at a small scale because they cannot finance:
inventory;
chicks or seed;
feed and fertiliser;
equipment;
transport;
storage;
additional staff;
processing capability.
Training may therefore create capable enterprises without giving them the financial capacity to grow.
5. Small agribusinesses are not presented as investable opportunities
The problem
A bank asks whether a borrower can repay. An investor asks whether a business can grow and produce a financial or measurable impact return.
Individual agripreneurs may be too small or too poorly documented to justify the cost of investor due diligence.
An investor would need evidence about:
revenue and margins;
historic growth;
customer retention;
production capacity;
capital requirements;
use of funds;
confirmed market demand;
key operational risks;
governance;
youth and employment outcomes;
women reached;
climate or regenerative-agriculture impact.
This information may exist across informal records, programme reports and conversations, but not as one investment-ready case.
Deeper root cause
The problem is partly one of transaction size and due-diligence economics.
An investor may be interested in agriculture and youth livelihoods but unable to justify conducting a full assessment on one very small agripreneur seeking a modest amount of capital.
An individual enterprise may be:
too small for institutional investment;
insufficiently formalised;
highly dependent on one person;
exposed to one buyer or one value chain;
unable to produce consistent reports;
unable to explain exactly how investment will create growth.
Portfolio problem
There may be dozens of potentially viable agripreneurs, but they are not organised into a portfolio with consistent records and comparable measures.
Consequently, an investor cannot easily answer:
What is the combined revenue?
How diversified is the portfolio?
How much capital is required?
Which enterprises are growth-ready?
What are the common risks?
What return or impact could the portfolio create?
How would performance be monitored after investment?
Programme consequence
Investment interest remains general rather than transactional. Investors may support the idea of youth agribusiness but still lack a sufficiently structured opportunity in which to invest.
6. Access to markets is unstable and reactive
The problem
Farmers and agripreneurs may produce without sufficient certainty about:
who will buy;
how much the buyer needs;
when the buyer needs it;
the required grade or quality;
the applicable price;
packaging requirements;
collection arrangements;
payment timing.
Buyer relationships may be informal, inconsistent or concentrated among a small number of traders.
Why this becomes serious
Agricultural products are time-sensitive.
If a buyer is not available when the produce is ready:
the farmer may accept a significantly lower price;
products may spoil;
storage costs may increase;
livestock may remain in production longer than planned;
feed and maintenance costs may continue;
repayment may be delayed;
the next production cycle may be disrupted.
Supply fragmentation
One farmer may have insufficient volume for a large buyer. At the same time, many nearby farmers may collectively have enough produce, but there is no reliable structure to combine:
production volume;
quality;
expected harvest date;
collection location;
buyer requirements.
Quality mismatch
Even when buyer demand exists, the farmer may produce something that does not meet the buyer's requirements.
For example:
the wrong variety;
inconsistent size;
inadequate packaging;
insufficient quantity;
missing certification;
delivery outside the required window.
The problem is therefore not only finding a buyer after production. It is coordinating production with market requirements before the farmer commits resources.
Programme consequence
Weak market coordination reduces:
farmer income;
enterprise sustainability;
confidence in agriculture as a livelihood;
ability to repay finance;
willingness to reinvest;
credibility of production forecasts.
7. Agricultural advice may be available but not sufficiently actionable
The problem
EKYAN already makes use of digital and AI-assisted agricultural advisory, including Ask Nia. The challenge is not the total absence of information. It is whether the advice is:
accurate;
current;
relevant to the correct value chain;
appropriate to the farmer's location;
sensitive to production stage;
available in an understandable language;
supported by reliable evidence;
clear about when professional intervention is needed.
Manuela Radelsboeck specifically raised the need for farmers and agripreneurs to receive accurate, quality information when they need it, including support with disease-related challenges. [UNICEF Gen...Recording | Video]
Why general advice can fail
A farmer may ask:
“Why are my chickens dying?”
A generic response may list several possible diseases, but the correct action depends on:
bird age;
symptoms;
mortality pattern;
recent vaccination;
feed;
water;
local disease outbreaks;
housing conditions;
veterinary history.
Giving an overconfident answer can be harmful.
Advice-action gap
Even correct advice may be unusable if the recommended action requires:
an unavailable medicine;
a qualified veterinarian;
money;
refrigeration;
laboratory testing;
transport;
specialist equipment.
Advice must therefore connect to the farmer's actual capacity to act.
Coverage problem
A tool may perform well for one area, such as poultry, but may not provide equivalent depth for:
horticulture;
maize;
dairy;
soil management;
post-harvest storage;
water management;
different local pests and diseases.
Programme consequence
Without appropriate controls, digital advisory can produce:
incorrect action;
delayed escalation;
unsafe pesticide or veterinary use;
loss of livestock or crops;
reduced trust in the agripreneur and programme.
8. Climate, water and soil constraints are locally specific
The problem
Agricultural recommendations cannot be applied uniformly across all counties and production contexts.
Participants face different combinations of:
unreliable rainfall;
drought;
flooding;
water scarcity;
soil degradation;
erosion;
pests and diseases;
temperature stress;
limited irrigation;
poor storage conditions.
The programme's experience in arid or pastoral settings cannot automatically be addressed with the same delivery model used in wetter, crop-focused areas.
Information timing problem
Weather and climate information may exist, but it is only useful if it reaches the farmer:
early enough;
in a localised form;
in a language they understand;
with a clear recommended action.
A forecast that says “heavy rainfall expected” is less useful than guidance such as:
Delay fertiliser application, clear drainage channels and move stored feed away from the floor.
Soil-information problem
Farmers may be advised to apply certain fertilisers or plant particular crops without sufficient knowledge of:
soil pH;
nutrient composition;
moisture retention;
prior land use;
local crop suitability.
Poorly targeted input use raises costs and may worsen soil conditions.
Programme consequence
Climate and soil uncertainty can undermine:
adoption;
productivity;
repayment ability;
buyer commitments;
farmer confidence;
the reliability of programme outcome projections.
9. The agripreneur role may become overloaded
The problem
Agripreneurs are central to EKYAN's operating model. They may be expected to:
advise farmers;
deliver training;
promote climate-smart practices;
record transactions;
support input access;
connect farmers to finance;
connect farmers to buyers;
maintain their own businesses;
report programme information;
follow up on farmer outcomes.
This makes the agripreneur both a local entrepreneur and an informal extension, coordination and data-collection point.
Scaling challenge
The programme reportedly intends to grow from approximately 512 agripreneurs toward a much larger network. Scaling participation does not automatically guarantee consistent service quality.
New agripreneurs may differ in:
technical capability;
business maturity;
digital literacy;
available time;
access to networks;
record-keeping discipline;
trust within local communities.
Administrative burden
If agripreneurs must enter the same information into multiple tools or reports, they may:
stop updating records;
submit incomplete data;
prioritise business activity over programme reporting;
keep important information in notebooks or WhatsApp chats;
report retrospectively and inaccurately.
Programme consequence
The programme's strongest local asset can become its largest operational bottleneck if agripreneurs are expected to coordinate everything without a simple structure and clear incentives.
10. The programme ecosystem is fragmented
The problem
EKYAN already works with several tools, organisations and delivery mechanisms. These may include:
Ask Nia;
OneNetwork;
Agribytes;
Leadership Academy;
Chomoka;
county government;
extension officers;
financial partners;
suppliers;
buyers;
schools and Centres of Excellence.
Each component may perform a valid role, but the participant's journey can remain fragmented.
What fragmentation looks like
A farmer may:
receive training in one channel;
ask advisory questions through another;
place an order informally;
save through a separate system;
obtain finance from another partner;
sell through a private buyer;
report outcomes in a periodic survey.
These interactions may not produce one coherent participant or enterprise history.
Deeper root cause
The shortcoming is not necessarily that individual tools do not work. It is that the programme may lack:
shared identifiers;
common data definitions;
connected workflows;
agreed escalation processes;
consistent consent rules;
clear ownership when a case moves between partners.
Programme consequence
Fragmentation creates:
participant repetition;
duplicated data capture;
inconsistent records;
limited traceability;
slower service;
difficult partner accountability;
weak end-to-end outcome measurement.
11. Programme evidence is largely retrospective
The problem
Programme teams need to know not only how many people were trained or reached, but what happened after support was delivered.
The current evidence model may be stronger at measuring:
training attendance;
participants reached;
services delivered;
intentions reported.
It is more difficult to prove continuously:
which blockers occurred;
who addressed them;
whether inputs were delivered;
whether the practice was implemented;
whether production improved;
whether income increased;
whether a job or sustainable enterprise resulted;
how much that result cost.
Output-versus-outcome problem
There is a material difference between:
Output: 1,000 participants were trained.
Intermediate outcome: 700 intended to implement.
Operational outcome: 500 obtained the necessary inputs.
Adoption outcome: 450 implemented the practice.
Economic outcome: 300 improved production or income.
Livelihood outcome: 150 sustained an enterprise or job.
If the programme only measures the first two levels, it cannot fully explain where value is created or lost.
Data consistency problem
Figures may differ between decks, reports and reporting periods because of:
different cut-off dates;
different participant definitions;
duplicate participants;
self-reported versus verified data;
changing denominators;
calculation or transcription errors.
This does not necessarily mean that the programme is performing poorly. It means the evidence model needs clearer definitions and lineage.
Programme consequence
Weak evidence affects:
donor confidence;
investment cases;
resource allocation;
scaling decisions;
comparison between counties or delivery methods;
programme learning.
12. Cost-effectiveness is difficult to determine
The problem
Manuela Radelsboeck indicated that targets concern both numbers and cost-effectiveness. [UNICEF Gen...Recording | Video]
It is relatively easy to calculate:
Total programme cost ÷ number of participants reached.
However, that does not show whether the participant:
adopted a practice;
increased production;
accessed finance;
reached a market;
earned more income;
created employment;
sustained the enterprise.
The denominator problem
“Cost per farmer reached” and “cost per farmer achieving a verified outcome” are fundamentally different measures.
A programme can reduce cost per person reached by delivering digital training at scale, while simultaneously reducing the proportion of people who receive enough follow-up to implement it.
Attribution problem
Many factors influence agricultural outcomes:
market prices;
weather;
county services;
private-sector participation;
household resources;
prior experience;
external programmes.
Therefore, EKYAN needs to distinguish between:
outputs directly delivered by the programme;
participant outcomes associated with the intervention;
outcomes that can reasonably be attributed to the programme.
Programme consequence
Without a stronger cost-and-outcome chain, scaling decisions may favour what is easy to count rather than what produces the greatest livelihood value.
13. Out-of-school youth outcomes remain uneven
The problem
The programme has reportedly achieved meaningful enterprise participation, but some employment and income targets have not been fully reached.
This suggests that training alone does not guarantee:
a job;
sufficient customers;
working capital;
a profitable enterprise;
a reliable market;
long-term business survival.
Pathway ambiguity
“Employment” and “entrepreneurship” are different outcomes.
An out-of-school young person may:
obtain formal employment;
become self-employed;
establish an agribusiness;
provide services to farmers;
participate seasonally;
remain trained but economically inactive.
If these pathways are not clearly defined and monitored separately, the programme may struggle to explain whether a participant's outcome is positive, incomplete or unsuccessful.
Structural constraints
Out-of-school youth may also have:
limited assets;
no land;
no collateral;
low savings;
limited professional networks;
household responsibilities;
transport constraints;
weaker access to established buyers.
Programme consequence
Young people may complete training but remain unable to convert capability into a stable livelihood.
14. Women and participants with disabilities face additional barriers
The problem
Equal access to training does not guarantee equal ability to benefit from it.
Young women may face:
limited ownership of land or assets;
care responsibilities;
reduced mobility;
time constraints;
limited access to collateral;
unequal decision-making power;
safety concerns;
concentration in lower-margin activities.
Participants with disabilities may face:
inaccessible interfaces;
unsuitable training formats;
transport barriers;
communication barriers;
exclusion by suppliers or financial institutions;
additional operating costs.
Hidden exclusion problem
A digital programme can appear inclusive because everyone is technically eligible, while still excluding people who lack:
smartphones;
data bundles;
reliable connectivity;
literacy;
digital confidence;
accessible technology.
Programme consequence
The overall programme average may improve while specific groups remain unable to progress from training to finance, markets and sustainable income.
15. Digital access and digital capability are uneven
The problem
Agripreneurs may use WhatsApp and mobile money extensively, but that does not mean all farmers have equivalent access or capability.
The programme must account for:
feature phones;
shared smartphones;
intermittent connectivity;
high data costs;
low digital literacy;
language barriers;
limited trust in unfamiliar systems;
inability to maintain passwords or accounts.
Agripreneur-versus-farmer distinction
Digital-use figures for agripreneurs must not automatically be applied to farmers.
Agripreneurs are selected and trained intermediaries. The farmer population may include people with significantly lower digital access.
Programme consequence
Designing only for direct smartphone use could exclude the farmers facing the greatest barriers and create a misleading picture of participation.
16. Value addition remains underdeveloped
The problem
Producing a primary agricultural product is only one part of the value chain.
Farmers and agripreneurs may capture more value through:
grading;
packaging;
storage;
processing;
aggregation;
preservation;
branding;
distribution.
However, value addition requires:
equipment;
quality standards;
reliable volume;
skills;
working capital;
energy;
storage;
market demand;
regulatory compliance.
Why it cannot simply be added as another feature
Processing without confirmed demand can create unsold stock. Equipment without sufficient volume can remain underused. Packaging without quality consistency may not unlock better prices.
Value addition is therefore not only a training problem. It is an enterprise and market coordination problem.
Programme consequence
Without viable value-addition pathways, participants remain concentrated in the lowest-margin part of the value chain.
17. The problems reinforce one another
These challenges should not be treated as separate items because they form a connected system.
For example:
A farmer receives poultry training.
The farmer cannot obtain vaccines.
Disease reduces production.
The farmer cannot fulfil the expected buyer quantity.
Revenue falls.
The farmer cannot repay or qualify for finance.
The next production cycle becomes smaller.
The programme records low adoption or weak income growth months later.
This is not one isolated “input problem.” It is a chain involving:
advisory;
inputs;
finance;
production risk;
buyer reliability;
evidence;
programme outcomes.
The deepest challenge EKYAN faces is therefore fragmented execution across the full participant journey.
Consolidated problem definition
A concise but complete problem statement would be:
EKYAN has created valuable training, agripreneur and partnership capabilities, but participants still lack a continuous operating structure that converts learning into implementation. Farmer blockers, input demand, business records, finance requirements, production forecasts, buyer opportunities and programme outcomes remain distributed across people, tools and periodic reports. As the programme scales, this fragmentation risks lowering adoption quality, limiting access to finance and markets, overloading agripreneurs and weakening evidence about cost-effectiveness and livelihood impact.
